## 4.6.8.1 What fabric sets by itself

fabric keeps policies of its own for every site and rewrites them on each apply: the trust in fabric's root CA, the
site's log-on policies (who may sign in, changed with the commands of 4.6.8.6) and its Windows baseline (4.6.7.1).
Leave those alone; everything else is yours, in the site's `fabric: <site> admin settings` policy, which fabric never touches except through the commands below (design:
[1.6.5.9](../volume_1_description_and_architecture/1.6.5-samba-dc.md#1659-group-policy-and-the-admx-editor)). Windows'
own tools (RSAT's Group Policy editor) work on the same domain too.

## 4.6.8.2 Loading templates

Policies come from ADMX templates. fabric does not ship Microsoft's (its licence does not allow it): download the
*Administrative Templates (.admx)* for your Windows version from Microsoft, then load the folder that holds the
`.admx` files and their `en-US` language folder:

```bash
sudo fabricctl gpo load /path/to/PolicyDefinitions
```

```bash
sudo fabricctl gpo templates
```

They go into the domain's central store, where Windows' own editor finds them as well.

## 4.6.8.3 Finding and setting policies

```bash
sudo fabricctl gpo list "screen saver"
```

lists the matching policies with their names, whether they are for machines or people, and their elements (the
values a policy takes: `text:…`, `decimal:…`, `boolean:…`, `enum:…`, `list:…`). Then:

```bash
sudo fabricctl gpo set <policy> <element>=<value>
```

- a number for `decimal`, any text for `text`, `on` or `off` for `boolean`, an option's name or number for `enum`,
  and `a;b;c` for `list`;
- `--disabled` sets the policy to *Disabled* instead;
- `sudo fabricctl gpo clear <policy>` puts it back to *Not configured*;
- `sudo fabricctl gpo show` lists everything the site's admin settings policy sets.

Machine policies reach the site's machines at their next policy refresh (`gpupdate`, or within about 90 minutes);
people's policies at their next log-on. Every change is in the audit log.

## 4.6.8.4 Your own templates

For settings of your own (a registry value your software reads), start from fabric's blank template:

```bash
sudo fabricctl gpo starter ~/my-policies
```

Rename the files and the namespace, replace the example policy with yours (each writes under
`Software\Policies\<your organisation>`), then `sudo fabricctl gpo load ~/my-policies`.

## 4.6.8.5 Controls the sites below cannot change

A site nested under yours inherits your `admin settings` as defaults: it may set the same policy differently, or block
inheritance on its OU (1.9.8.14). For a setting the sites below must keep, set it as a **control** instead:

```bash
sudo fabricctl gpo set <policy> <element>=<value> --control
```

It goes into your site's `fabric: <site> controls` policy, which is linked *enforced*: it applies to your site and
every site below it, wins over their own policies and passes their blocked inheritance. `gpo show --control` lists
them and `gpo clear <policy> --control` removes one. In the web UI (Directory, Group Policy) the controls are listed
apart, and a policy is set as one with *As a control (enforced)*.

## 4.6.8.6 Who may sign in to the machines

Who may sign in to a joined machine is the log-on policy of the OU it sits in; an OU without one of its own takes its
parent's (design: [1.6.11.4](../volume_1_description_and_architecture/1.6.11-host-access.md#16114-who-may-sign-in-per-ou)).
Each policy names the groups allowed **at the keyboard** and **remotely** (RDP, and SSH on Linux through sssd).
`fabric-break-glass` and each machine's own local Administrators are always allowed as well, so you cannot lock
yourself out. A new install starts with `<site>-machine-admins` on the site's `OU=machines`; an upgraded one keeps the
rights it had before 0.9 (the site's people and admins) until you change them.

See each OU's policy (also in `sudo fabricctl domain status`):

```bash
sudo fabricctl domain logon show
```

Let a group sign in to the machines of an OU (a path below your site's OU: `machines`, `machines/servers/linux`, a sub-OU you
made; `site` for the site's OU itself). Groups are separated by commas; a right you leave out stays as it is:

```bash
sudo fabricctl domain logon set machines/servers/linux --console lan-machine-admins,lan-devs --remote lan-devs
```

Let an OU take its parent's policy again:

```bash
sudo fabricctl domain logon clear machines/servers/linux
```

Each change is saved and applied at once (`--no-apply` only saves it); the OU's policy is the GPO
`fabric: <site> log-on <OU>`, linked to that OU. Refused, with the reason: a group the domain does not have, an OU
path that is not one, the site's `OU=machines` (or its OU) granting nobody, and a clear that would leave the site's
machines without any policy of fabric's. An OU that does not exist yet is saved, and the apply says it skipped it
until the OU is made. The first change on an upgraded site keeps its old rights as the site OU's policy, so only what
you changed changes.

In the web console: **Directory → Machines → Who may sign in** lists each OU's policy, with **Clear** beside each and a
form to set one (OU, the groups at the keyboard, the groups remotely). Changing it needs the permission `gpo:admin`.
Windows applies a change at its next policy refresh (`gpupdate /force` at once); Linux machines when sssd next reads
the policy (a few minutes; restarting sssd reads it at once).
